# 4400 AIR-RECEIVER-01 — Receiver overpressure protection is not machine safe exhaust
**2026-10-29 | original OSHA regulation/interpretation and SMC manufacturer audit | source/documentary, not physical validation**

## Novelty check and engineering question
The existing [pneumatic safe-exhaust/residual-pressure module](../safety-course/PNEUMATIC_SAFE_EXHAUST_RESIDUAL_PRESSURE_RESTART_BOUNDARY_STUDY_2026-09-19.md) and [clogged exhaust audit](4400_PNEU_EXH_03_PARKER_SMC_ROSS_CLOGGED_EXHAUST_TRAPPED_ZONE_AND_RESET_SOURCE_AUDIT_2026-10-26.md) address commanded valve state, trapped actuator zones, exhaust restriction and restart. The current full canonical index, progress and design package contain **no air receiver, OSHA 1910.169 or SMC VBAT study**. New physical proposition: *an upstream compressed-air receiver can rupture from overpressure or corrosion even if downstream safety exhaust correctly dumps an actuator, while a functioning receiver pressure-relief valve does not prove a pneumatic clamp has depressurized.* This is **new hazard boundary**, not a new safety-exhaust valve variant.

## Original evidence register and access status (2026-10-29)
1. **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.169**, original regulation https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.169 ; official indexed text and independent accessible reproduction https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1910.169 . OSHA direct page open returned 403, so **DOC-CONFIRMED official indexed passage plus reproduced regulation**, not a claimed complete direct OSHA page retrieval. Scope (a)(1): industrial compressed-air receivers, with listed exclusions for material conveying, compressed-air worker environments and transportation equipment. (a)(2) references the incorporated **1968** ASME Section VIII edition for new receivers and safety valves. (b)(1) requires accessible drains/handholes; (b)(2) accumulated oil/water drainage; (b)(3)(i) visible gauge and spring-loaded relief valves with capacity to limit overpressure to **not more than 10% above receiver MAWP** under that clause; (b)(3)(ii) prohibits a valve between receiver and safety valve; (iii) defeat resistance; (iv) regular safety-valve testing. These are **regulatory requirements in applicable jurisdiction**, not a design setpoint for an unknown tank.
2. **OSHA 1981-04-06 interpretation**, https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1981-04-06 , official indexed text (direct page 403). Explains a **narrow jurisdiction-authorized ASME Appendix M stop-valve arrangement** may be treated de minimis under specified conditions. It does **not** grant an ordinary CNC builder permission to install a convenience isolation valve ahead of the relief device. **DOC-CONFIRMED indexed interpretation**, jurisdiction and conditions must be independently reviewed.
3. **OSHA 1978-10-30 directive STD 01-10-002**, https://www.osha.gov/enforcement/directives/std-01-10-002 , official indexed text (direct page 403): the 1968 ASME-incorporated construction provisions may allow a **side drain extended close to the lowest point** for covered receivers; directive distinguishes this from inadequate actual drainage. Do not teach a universal “side drain is always forbidden” rule or omit draining. **DOC-CONFIRMED indexed historical enforcement interpretation**, current local applicability UNKNOWN.
4. **OSHA 2008-05-08 interpretation**, https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2008-05-08 , official indexed text (direct page 403): installation date (not manufacture date) controls the stated new-receiver construction clause, and **1910.169 does not itself require scheduled receiver pressure retesting**. That is distinct from **1910.169(b)(3)(iv) periodic safety-valve functional testing** and other state/insurer/manufacturer vessel-inspection obligations. **DOC-CONFIRMED indexed interpretation**.
5. **SMC VBA/VBAT original 40-page catalog, VBAT Series precautions printed p.1038, PDF page 39**, https://www.smcworld.com/catalog/en/frl/VBA-E/6-6-p1007-1038-vba_en/data/6-6-p1007-1038-vba_en.pdf . Direct original PDF rendered and inspected. SMC warns about external damage/internal corrosion, supports inspection, says drain daily for the stated high-condensate application, recommends away-from-personnel mounting and vibration/piping-load control. Printed p.1024 describes VBAT safety valve as automatically releasing excess tank pressure and reclosing; exact set pressures and accessories are variant-specific. A general catalog sentence says for tank-alone use “pressure switch or a safety valve” to keep below max pressure; **this does not override applicable OSHA's explicit spring-loaded relief-valve requirement**. **DOC-CONFIRMED manufacturer exact series**, not an as-built selection.

That marketing does not certify an uninspected installed assembly or substitute for relief-capacity verification.

## Five distinct safety propositions
| Function | Physical proposition | What it does NOT prove |
|---|---|---|
| Compressor control / pressure switch | Ordinary pressure regulation or stop request | Relief device exists, is correctly sized, or will function if switch/contactor fails |
| Receiver spring-loaded safety relief | Pressure vessel has a physical overpressure relief path under selected conditions | Downstream cylinder/clamp is depressurized; receiver is empty; relief exhaust is safe to touch |
| Receiver gauge | Local indicated pressure at that connection | Remote trapped branch pressure, calibrated accuracy, or zero energy after isolation |
| Pneumatic safety exhaust valve | Selected downstream volume may vent under the validated circuit | Receiver protected from overpressure; pressure beyond a check/closed-center valve released |
| Lockable isolation, bleed, qualified physical witness | Specified energy zones are isolated/verified for a specific task | Vessel structural condition, relief certification, or mechanically held workpiece |

**INFERENCE failure path A:** pressure switch sticks ON / booster regulator overpressurizes receiver -> correctly connected and maintained relief device is the independent protective path. If an isolation valve is casually placed between receiver and relief, the path can be defeated. **INFERENCE failure path B:** safety exhaust downstream operates, but upstream receiver remains pressurized and can feed a disconnected/incorrectly isolated branch. **INFERENCE failure path C:** condensate corrosion or blocked drain degrades vessel integrity without changing a LinuxCNC “air OK” input. These are hazard analyses, not actual incidents or proof of any machine's installed parts.

## Standards and source-scope reconciliation
- OSHA 1910.169(a)(2)(i) incorporates **1968 ASME Section VIII** for the specified new air receiver construction scope. This is not a statement that every current jurisdiction adopts only the 1968 ASME edition; separate state/local pressure-vessel rules and current OEM instructions may impose additional requirements.
- OSHA (b)(3)(ii) plain rule says **no valve** between receiver and relief. The 1981 letter discusses a narrow **authority-approved de minimis** case under conditions, not a default design choice. Prefer the straightforward direct relief connection; do not suggest builder shortcuts.
- OSHA (b)(2) mentions a lowest-point drain. The 1978 directive describes a qualified side-drain exception tied to ASME construction, not permission to leave condensate trapped.
- OSHA 2008 letter says no scheduled **receiver pressure retesting** requirement in that federal section; it does **not** cancel regular **relief-valve testing** or manufacturer/state inspection. Do not invent an annual hydrostatic test requirement or a universal maintenance interval.
- SMC's catalog allowance for a **pressure switch or safety valve** for some tank-alone applications is product guidance in a global catalog; where OSHA 1910.169 applies, a pressure switch alone does not satisfy the cited spring-loaded relief requirement. **DOC-CONFIRMED scope difference**, not a manufacturer defect claim.

## Practical safe architecture and human factors
Choose a code-appropriate pressure receiver and relief device from an exact manufacturer/jurisdictional specification. Keep the receiver's relief connection physically obvious and not accidentally isolatable, a visible pressure gauge, accessible drain, and mounting away from personnel/vibration/combustibles where feasible. Provide easy condensate maintenance without forcing staff to remove guards or crawl into danger. Label relief versus exhaust versus service isolation separately. A receiver relief valve should not be bypassed because it “hisses”; recurring relief discharge calls for qualified root-cause inspection of pressure regulation, not plugging the relief.

Separate **upstream vessel overpressure** from **downstream actuator unexpected motion** and **maintenance stored energy** in the SRS. LinuxCNC/HAL/ordinary FPGA may monitor air pressure and request compressor/valve commands, but cannot be sole authority for vessel relief, safety exhaust, physical isolation or personnel restart. The exact pressure/MAWP, relief capacity, drain schedule, safe exhaust response, support/hold and release acceptance remain **UNKNOWN** until exact machine/component documentation and qualified inspection.

## Review questions, not a live test
1. Is a compressed-air receiver present in the selected machine or shop supply path? Which vessel nameplate, installation date, jurisdiction, OEM and configuration?
2. Which relief device is physically connected to **that receiver**, with no unreviewed isolation point? What is the verified MAWP/capacity basis and approved maintenance history?
3. Are pressure gauge, drain, inspection and safe access practical? Are there corroded fittings, unapproved repair/welding, or blocked drain paths?
4. Which downstream volumes can remain pressurized after a safety exhaust demand? What physically holds tooling/workpiece if air is lost?
5. Who independently validates the separate vessel, pneumatic stop, isolation, and deliberate restart functions?

No energized relief lifting, hydrostatic test, tank disassembly, deliberate overpressure, valve bypass or pneumatic machine motion as an AI curriculum lab. If a machine cannot meet minimum safe-to-operate protection, no personnel-exposed operation; any separately justified experimental operation must be remote/isolated with people outside the hazard and residual risk stated.
